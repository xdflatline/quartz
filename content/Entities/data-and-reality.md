## Overview

*Data and Reality* is a short, philosophically dense book on the gap between the real world and the data models we impose on it. William Kent argues that all data structures — hierarchical, network, relational, object-oriented — are *maps*, and that the territory (information as it actually exists among people) is amorphous, view-dependent, and not fully capturable by any formal system. The book alternates between chapters on real-world information (entities, identity, naming, relationships) and chapters on data-processing models in the computer.

Despite early critical acclaim the book was a "sleeper for over twenty years" (Kent, 1998 Preface). It was republished unchanged in 1998 because, in Kent's view, a revised edition would miss the point: the book's questions are timeless and orthogonal to whichever storage technology happens to dominate.

## Key Details

- **First edition:** North Holland, 1978
- **Reissue:** 1stBooks, 1998 — adds a Preface to the Second Edition and explicit acknowledgement that some sections prefigure object orientation
- **Structure (per the original preface):** Ch 1 in the world of real information; Ch 2 visits formally structured information systems; Ch 3–6 on other aspects of real information; Ch 7–11 on data-processing models; Ch 12 philosophical observations (the source of all the excerpts reproduced on bkent.net)
- **Scope:** limited to information that is "managed in most current files and data bases" — large quantity, permanent, simplistic structure (personnel files, bank records, inventory). Excludes AI, natural language, prose, performance, transactions, integrity, recovery, security.
- **Influences cited:** Hayakawa (map/territory), Zemanek (philosophical observations), Mealy (theory of data), Whorf (linguistic relativity), Sapir, Thomas (the human as symbiosis), Trivers (sociobiology), Jastrow (AI/human convergence), and S. I. Hayakawa's *Language in Thought and Action*

## Reception

Universal praise from database researchers in the late 1970s and early 1980s; sustained citations by Celko (1998), Meersman (1998), Burkhart (1998), Kilov (1999), and Soley (1999). The book is repeatedly described as "foundational" — not oriented to a particular technology — and as one whose central problems (viewpoints, ambiguity, naming) remain unresolved in modern systems.

## Key claims in the excerpts

- **No adequate formal modelling system exists** — information is too amorphous, ambiguous, subjective to be pinned down deterministically.
- **Data models are tools, not theories** — incomplete, economic, orthogonal to the problems they solve. Users adapt to tools, not the reverse.
- **Map vs. territory** — the title's central analogy: highways are not painted red.
- **Three worlds of modelling** — reality (the territory), the language we use to describe it (which is itself a map), and the computer system (another map of a map of the territory).
- **Brain-style preference for data model** — debates over relational vs. hierarchical vs. network may track cognitive style (visual / auditory / network-oriented thinkers), not objective merit.
- **Linguistic relativity of modelling** — vocabulary determines which phenomena appear as singular entities or relationships.
- **Reconciliation by scope and purpose** — sharing a common view of reality is high for narrow purposes and few people, low for broad purposes and many people; technology increases scope, exposing the gap.
